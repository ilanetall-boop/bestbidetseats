Same Bidet Seat, Different Brand Name: Spotting a Rebadged Listing

Search "electric bidet toilet seat" on Amazon long enough and a pattern starts to repeat: two listings, two different brand names at the top of the page, and — once you scroll past the hero photo — the exact same bullet points, in the exact same order, describing the exact same features in the exact same words. Sometimes the product photos match too, right down to the second and third image in the gallery. This isn't a coincidence, and it isn't a violation of anything. It's what a rebadged listing looks like, and once you know the signs, you'll see it constantly in this category.

We pulled five real listings for this guide — three sold under the name mijashy and two under the name IYTATA — priced from $129.99 to $143.99. We didn't go looking for a dramatic exposé; we went looking at our own product database for a clean, checkable example of how common rebadging actually is, and these five gave us one without any editorializing required. What follows is what the published bullet points and photos on these five listings actually say, what that tells you about who makes them, and — the part that matters if you're the one buying — when paying more for the "other" brand name gets you nothing at all.

One honest note before we start: we're comparing published specifications, prices and listing details, not physically disassembling five bidet seats to check for a shared circuit board. We don't run a testing lab and we're not going to pretend otherwise. Where a spec isn't published for one of these five — a warranty term, a verified rating, a bundle contents list — we say so rather than guess.

The short answer

If you just want the verdict

Rebadging isn't a scam by itself

Same hardware, different sticker

A handful of contract factories build the bidet seat hardware sold under dozens of storefront names, and two "brands" sharing identical bullet copy usually just means they're sourcing the same base unit. That's a normal, legal part of how marketplace electronics get made — the problem only starts when a seller charges a premium for a name with nothing behind it.

Buy on the spec sheet, not the name

Compare bullets before you compare logos

When two listings publish the same feature list, the brand name at the top tells you almost nothing about which one to buy — the price does. In the five listings below, the cheapest one that matches your bowl shape is very often the correct pick, full stop.

The tell

How to spot a rebadged listing

The giveaway is rarely the product itself — it's the copy describing it. Here's exactly what we found across the five listings in our database. All three mijashy listings carry this bullet, word for word: "ADJUSTABLE WATER PRESSURE - Enjoy a customized clean with stainless steel nozzles that offer adjustable water pressure and dual wash modes (front wash & rear wash). The self-cleaning nozzles ensure hygiene, while the oscillating functions provide a spa-like experience." Now look at the two IYTATA listings: the identical sentence, unchanged down to the punctuation. A third bullet — "LUXURY COMFORT FEATURES," covering the heated seat, soft-close lid, wireless remote and nightlight — is also identical across all five, mijashy and IYTATA alike. The middle bullet, about warm water and the air dryer, is identical on four of the five; the fifth swaps "adjustable settings" for "adjustable water temperature," a rewrite that changes nothing about what the feature does.

The photos tell the same story. Checking the image sets attached to each of these five listings, the second and third product photos are the same files, reused across all five — two different brand names, one shared set of studio shots. A fourth photo repeats across four of the five; the exception is the one round-bowl IYTATA listing, which swaps in a photo specific to a round seat instead. Only the very first photo — the main listing image — is unique to each one, which tracks: that's the one image a seller typically commissions or edits themselves, while the rest of the gallery is supplied as-is by whoever manufactures the unit.

01

Bullet points, word for word

Copy the longest, most specific bullet on a listing you're considering and paste it into Amazon's search bar. If it turns up under a second brand name unchanged, you've found the same base hardware.

02

The exact same photos

Open the full image gallery, not just the hero shot. If the second, third or fourth photo is pixel-identical to one on a competing listing, both are almost certainly supplied by the same factory.

03

A shared model code

Some listing titles include an internal model number alongside the marketing name — "102," "102D." When that number shows up on two different brands' listings, it's the factory's own part number leaking through the rebadge.

04

A brand name with one product

Click through to the storefront. A brand selling exactly one bidet seat, with no other listings and no independent website, is consistent with a seller who licensed a factory's stock unit and gave it a name — not evidence of anything worse, just a sign to verify everything else yourself.

Why this happens

Same factory, different seller account

The economics

One factory, many storefronts

A relatively small number of contract manufacturers build the bulk of the sub-$200 electric bidet seat hardware sold on Amazon. A factory sells the same base unit — nozzles, heater, control board, remote — to any importer who orders a container, and that importer registers their own Amazon brand name to list it. The bullet copy and product photography frequently ship as part of the same package deal, which is exactly why they show up unchanged across storefronts.

Why sellers do it

A registered brand unlocks the listing tools

Amazon's Brand Registry gives sellers access to enhanced content, protection against unauthorized resellers, and a cleaner listing page — advantages a generic, unbranded product page doesn't get. Registering a name costs little and takes weeks, so two importers who bought from the same factory each register their own name rather than list under one shared brand, even when the underlying seat is the same.

None of this is against Amazon's rules, and it isn't inherently dishonest — a factory selling its own hardware to multiple buyers is how most consumer electronics reach a shelf, branded or not. mijashy and IYTATA, based on what's published for these five listings, each look like single-line importers rather than manufacturers in their own right: one product family, no independent brand history we could find, and a naming convention — "102," "102D" — that reads like an internal factory model code rather than a marketing name. That's a normal way for this corner of the marketplace to work. It just means the two names on these listings are less informative than they look, and the actual decision should rest on the bullet points and the price, which brings us to the part that matters for your wallet.

The trap

When a higher price on a clone listing is just a worse deal

The pattern worth internalizing from these five listings isn't "cheaper is always better" — it's that a higher price on a rebadged listing needs to be justified by something you can actually point to, and often it isn't. Here, the $143.99 mijashy elongated listing publishes the exact same three feature bullets as the $129.99 mijashy elongated listing. Same brand, same features, $40 apart. That's the textbook version of a worse deal: nothing in the published spec sheet explains the gap, which means the extra $40 is buying the longer title and nothing else we can verify.

Compare that to a price difference that is justified: paying more for a listing with an instant water heater instead of a reservoir tank, a stainless steel wand instead of plastic, or a manufacturer that stocks replacement parts years after you buy. Those are real, checkable differences that show up in the spec sheet or the brand's own track record. None of that is present across these five — the difference is a brand name and, in one case, a slightly longer title describing features the cheaper listing already has.

The practical rule: before paying more for one "brand" over a similarly specced one, find the specific feature, material, or warranty term that costs the extra money. If you can name it and confirm it's actually absent from the cheaper option, the premium is earned. If the only difference you can find is the name at the top of the page, you're paying for a sticker — and on a $130–$170 purchase, that sticker tax adds up to real money for genuinely nothing. FAQ

Rebadged listings: common questions

Is it illegal or against Amazon's rules for two brands to sell the same product?

No. A factory selling the same base hardware to multiple importers, each of whom registers their own Amazon brand name, is a normal and legal part of how marketplace electronics reach shelves. It becomes a problem only if a seller misrepresents the product's origin or copies another brand's protected trademark or packaging outright — neither of which is what's happening when two listings simply share supplier-provided bullet copy and photography.

If two listings share identical bullet points, are they guaranteed to be the exact same hardware?

Identical bullet copy is strong evidence of a shared factory or supplier, since that text is usually provided by the manufacturer as part of the listing package — but it isn't absolute proof the internal components are byte-for-byte identical. A factory can build the same shell to a stricter or looser spec depending on what a given importer pays for. Treat matching bullets as a reason to expect similar performance, not a certified guarantee, and check the price and any available reviews before assuming total equivalence.

Why would I ever pay more for the pricier of two nearly identical listings?

Sometimes there's a real reason — a live promotion structure, Prime shipping speed, a seller's return-handling reputation, or simply that the cheaper listing goes out of stock. None of those reasons live in the published feature bullets, though, so they're worth confirming on the live listing rather than assuming the higher price buys better hardware by default.

How do I check if a listing I'm looking at is rebadged?

Copy a distinctive, longer bullet point from the listing — not a generic phrase like "warm water" — and paste it into Amazon's search bar or a general web search. If the same sentence turns up under a different brand name, you've found its sibling listing. Then compare the price and any stated bowl-fit difference before deciding which one to buy.
