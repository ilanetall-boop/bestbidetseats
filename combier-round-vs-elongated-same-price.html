Shop for an electric bidet seat long enough and you start to expect a round-bowl tax, or an elongated-bowl tax, depending on the brand. Some charge $20–$30 more for one shape than the other; some charge the same. We've documented the inconsistency across four other listings and found no industry rule at all — just whatever each manufacturer decided. Combier's pair is the cleanest example of a brand that decided the shape shouldn't matter to the price: the elongated model and the round model both list at $143.99.

That's useful, because it strips out the variable that usually does most of the deciding for people. When one bowl shape costs $30 more, plenty of shoppers quietly let the cheaper option win even when it's the wrong fit. Combier removes that shortcut. With price identical on both sides, what's left is the question you should have been answering first anyway: which one physically fits your toilet, and does anything else about the two seats actually differ once you set bowl shape aside.

One honest note before we get into it: we don't run a fake testing lab, and we never claim to. Everything below comes from comparing the two product listings themselves, Combier's published specs, and the pattern we've researched across other same-brand round/elongated pairs.

Why this is unusual

Most brands charge differently for round and elongated. Combier doesn't.

Elongated bowls and round bowls aren't the same amount of seat. An elongated seat is a few inches longer, uses more plastic and a longer heating element path, and — because elongated is the dominant shape in new US construction — tends to be the shape a brand designs around first, with round added as a variant later. That's part of why you'll sometimes see a price gap: the elongated model is the "main" product and the round one is treated as a line extension with its own small margin decision.

Other brands price it the opposite way, charging more for round because it's the lower-volume shape and costs more per unit to tool and stock. We looked at four listings in a separate comparison and found both patterns in the wild, with no consistent direction — some brands charge more for elongated, some for round, and a few charge the same. Combier falls into that last group. Both of its electric seats are listed at exactly $143.99, and the database shows no asterisk or bundle difference explaining a gap that isn't there.

What that tells you about Combier specifically, and not about the category generally, is covered further down. For now, the practical point is this: because Combier isn't using price to nudge you toward either shape, the decision falls entirely on fit — which is exactly where it belongs.

Step one, always

Confirm your own bowl shape before either model is even relevant

Neither of these two seats is a universal fit, and no electric bidet seat on the market is. Before you compare features, pros, or anything else between the round and elongated Combier models, you need one piece of information: which shape your toilet actually is. Skip this step and you can end up with a seat that's visibly too short or too long for the bowl, with a gap at the front or back that no amount of bracket adjustment fixes.

The quick way to tell the two shapes apart without a tape measure: stand over the bowl and look at the front edge. An elongated bowl comes to more of a point, egg-shaped and visibly longer front to back — roughly 18.5 inches from the hinge bolts to the front rim. A round bowl is shorter and more circular, usually landing around 16.5 inches from hinge bolts to front rim. If you want to be precise rather than eyeball it, measure from the two hinge bolts at the back of the seat to the front-most point of the bowl. The elongated Combier's listed size is 15" x 20" — that's the seat's overall footprint, not the hinge-to-front measurement, so use it as a sanity check on scale rather than the primary number.

If you're replacing an existing seat rather than installing on a bare bowl, the fastest check is simpler still: take the old seat off and look at it. Round seats are noticeably shorter and more oval; elongated seats are longer and narrower at the front. When in doubt, measure the bowl itself rather than trust a memory of "I think it's standard." Plenty of owners have ordered the wrong shape because they assumed their toilet matched whatever they'd had before, in a previous home, with a different builder.

With price pulled off the table, bowl shape and size are the only hard constraint left, and they're not negotiable the way a feature preference is. A seat built for a round bowl will not sit flush on an elongated one, and vice versa — you'll get an overhang or a gap, uneven weight distribution on the mounting bracket, and in some cases a lid that doesn't close flush against the bowl rim. This is a measure-once-order-once decision. There's no "close enough" with bowl shape the way there might be with nozzle pressure settings or remote button layout.

Practically: measure your bowl using the hinge-bolt-to-front-rim method above, compare it against the elongated Combier's stated 15" x 20" footprint, and if your toilet reads shorter and rounder, go with the round model instead. If you're still unsure after measuring, Combier's own listings and Amazon's fit-finder tools (where available on the product page) are the next check — not a guess based on what "most toilets" are.

What it says about the brand

What a same-price round/elongated pair tells you about a brand's manufacturing

Pricing both bowl shapes identically is a small signal, but it's a real one. It suggests Combier's elongated and round shells cost roughly the same to produce — same internal wash and dry hardware, same remote, same soft-close hinge mechanism, with only the shell mold and mounting geometry differing between them. When a manufacturer treats the two shapes as cosmetic variants of one underlying platform rather than two separately-engineered products, equal pricing is the natural result. It's a sign of a mature, standardized production line rather than one shape being a bolted-on afterthought.

Contrast that with brands that charge a premium for one shape over the other. A price gap can mean the pricier shape genuinely costs more to tool — more material, a different heating element run, lower production volume — or it can simply mean the brand is charging what the market will bear for the less common shape, where buyers have fewer alternatives to compare against. Our look at four other round/elongated listings found real gaps in both directions, which is itself evidence that the price difference (when it exists) is a business decision more than an engineering necessity. Combier choosing not to charge one is consistent with a feature set that reads identically between the two listings — wash, dry, soft-close, remote, on both — rather than a round model quietly getting less for the same money, or vice versa.

None of that is a guarantee of equal build quality — we don't have independent teardown data on either Combier seat, and we're not going to claim one. It's simply the most consistent read of what the pricing and the two listings, taken together, actually show: a brand that built one platform in two shapes rather than two different products that happen to share a name.

FAQ

Combier round vs elongated: common questions

Why do Combier's round and elongated bidet seats cost the same?

Both list at $143.99 because Combier appears to treat round and elongated as shell variants of the same underlying platform — same wash, dry, soft-close and remote hardware — rather than separately priced products. Other brands do charge differently for the two shapes; Combier is simply one that doesn't.

How do I know if my toilet is round or elongated?

Measure from the two hinge bolts at the back of the seat to the front-most point of the bowl. Elongated bowls run roughly 18.5 inches and look more egg-shaped and pointed at the front; round bowls run roughly 16.5 inches and look shorter and more circular. If you're replacing an existing seat, you can also just look at its shape directly.

Is the round Combier model missing any features the elongated one has?

Based on the two listings, no — both specify wash, dry, soft-close lid and remote control. The round model's name explicitly includes "Heated"; the elongated listing's title doesn't carry that word, which is worth checking directly on that listing if heated water is a priority for you, since a title isn't a full spec sheet.

Can I use the elongated Combier seat on a round bowl, or vice versa?

No. Electric bidet seats are molded to a specific bowl shape and mounting geometry. Using the wrong shape typically leaves a gap or overhang, uneven seating on the mounting bracket, and a lid that may not close flush. Measure your bowl before ordering either model.
