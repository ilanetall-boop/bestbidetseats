Bidet Seats That Aren't White: How Limited Your Color Options Really Are

By Ilane TallUpdated October 22, 2026Research-based

Shop for a bidet seat for more than ten minutes and a pattern becomes obvious: almost everything on the page is white. Not off-white, not a close match, not "white-ish" — the same bright, cold, hospital-fixture white, listing after listing, brand after brand. If your toilet happens to be that exact shade, you'll never notice. If it isn't — if you own a bone, almond, or biscuit-colored toilet from the 1990s or early 2000s, or a colored fixture from further back — you'll notice immediately, because almost nothing in the category is built to match it.

This isn't an oversight anyone is actively trying to fix. It's the predictable result of how the bidet seat industry built itself around a market that had already gone almost entirely white. In our own catalogue, out of the manual and electric bidet seats we track, exactly one listing is sold in anything other than white: Kohler's PureWash M250, available in a shade called Biscuit. That single exception is the whole story of this article — what Biscuit actually buys you, how it compares to the identical seat in white, and what happens to your color options the moment you want electric features layered on top.

One honest note before we get into it: we don't run a testing lab, and we haven't sat on either of these seats ourselves. Everything below comes from what each Amazon listing states about color, fit and features, plus current pricing — if a listing doesn't say it, we're not going to guess at it either.

The default

Why bright white became the bidet-seat industry's only color

The simplest explanation is also the correct one: toilets went white, and bidet seats followed. American toilet manufacturers spent the back half of the twentieth century offering genuine color choice — avocado, harvest gold, pink, blue, and the softer "builder" shades like bone, almond and biscuit that showed up in most mid-range bathrooms through the 1980s and 90s. Trends moved on, white became the overwhelming default for new construction and renovation alike, and the toilets being installed today are white to a degree that makes any other color a special order rather than a standard stock item.

A bidet seat is a secondary product sold against that primary one, and secondary products follow the primary market's center of gravity. If the enormous majority of toilets being bought right now are white, a manufacturer gets the most sales for the least tooling and inventory complexity by making one shell color and selling it everywhere. Building a second color means a second mold run, a second SKU to warehouse, and a second listing to maintain — all to serve a shrinking pool of buyers whose fixture predates the white-only era. For most brands, that math simply doesn't clear. It's not that off-white bidet seats are hard to engineer; it's that stocking them for a shrinking customer base is a bet most manufacturers have decided isn't worth making.

That's what makes a listing like Kohler's Biscuit M250 worth a closer look. Kohler is a plumbing fixture manufacturer first — it has sold toilets, sinks and tubs in Biscuit, Almond and other builder shades for decades, alongside white, because the fixture side of its business never fully abandoned those colors. A seat brand with no fixture history has no reason to stock a second shell color. A brand that already tools and warehouses Biscuit for toilets and tanks has a much shorter path to offering it on a seat too.

The one exception

Kohler's Biscuit M250: the listing built for anything but bright white

The Kohler PureWash M250 Manual Elongated Bidet Toilet Seat, Biscuit ($117.30) is, as far as our catalogue shows, the only bidet seat built specifically to sit next to a bone, almond or off-white toilet without looking like a mismatched replacement part. It's a manual seat — no pump, no heater, no cord — which keeps the mechanism itself simple: household water pressure feeds a retractable nozzle, controlled by a dial or lever rather than a remote. Its listing describes a quiet-close lid designed to prevent slamming, cool-water operation, an easy installation built around quick-release hinges, and adjustable spray position and pressure. It fits elongated bowls, which covers most toilets installed in the US since the 1990s.

What the listing does not claim matters just as much as what it does. There's no mention of a heater, a dryer, a remote, or any of the comfort features that show up on electric seats — this is a non-electric bidet seat whose entire differentiator, relative to the rest of the market, is its shade. If you've already read our piece on warm-water claims, you'll recognize the shape of this trade-off: a manual seat gives you cold water and a mechanical dial in exchange for simplicity, a lower price, and in this one case, a color that isn't bright white. Biscuit isn't a bonus layered on top of an otherwise-identical electric seat — it's bundled specifically with the simpler mechanism, which is the honest reason it exists at this price rather than at electric-seat prices.

Same seat, different shade

Comparing the Biscuit M250 to its own white sibling

Kohler also sells the identical PureWash M250 mechanism in standard white, and the two listings read almost like a controlled experiment in what color actually costs you. The Kohler PureWash M250 Manual Round Bidet Toilet Seat, White ($116.26) carries the same quiet-close lid built to prevent slamming, the same quick-release hinges for easy installation, and the same adjustable spray position and pressure as the Biscuit version. The price gap between them is $1.04 — close enough to call it a rounding error rather than a color premium.

The one spec where the two listings in our catalogue genuinely diverge, beyond the color itself, is bowl fit: the white M250 we carry is built for a round bowl, while the Biscuit M250 is built for an elongated one. That's worth being precise about, because it means these two particular listings aren't a pure like-for-like swap — you can't simply pick whichever color matches your bathroom and assume the fit is interchangeable. If your toilet is round and you need the off-white shade, or elongated and you specifically want white, check Kohler's current listings for that exact pairing before ordering; our data only confirms these two specific combinations. What the two listings do confirm, reliably, is that within the M250 line, color is not gatekeeping features or mechanism — quiet-close, quick-release hinges and adjustable spray carry over regardless of shade. The thing you're actually choosing between is bowl shape and shade together, not comfort versus color.

Color and electric, together

What happens once you want color and electric features together

Here's where the Biscuit M250's exception stops helping you. The moment you move from a manual seat to an electric one — the category with a heater, a pump, a remote and the features most buyers actually want — color choice disappears from our catalogue entirely. The Bidet Toilet Seat with Warm Water Wash, Air Dryer, Heated Seat, Remote Control, LED Display, Self-Cleaning Nozzle, Night Light & Multiple Cleaning Modes ($199.99), sold for round bowls, is a good example of why: its own listing doesn't specify a color field at all in our data. That's not us assuming it's white and moving on — it's genuinely unlisted, and if color matters to you, confirming it directly on the current Amazon listing before buying is the only honest way to know.

That absence is itself informative. Manual seats like the M250 are mechanically simple enough that a second shell color is a modest manufacturing decision. Electric seats carry a heater, a circuit board, a pump, sensors and a remote receiver inside that shell — more tooling, more injection-molded parts, more SKUs to validate if a second color were added. Almost every electric seat we've covered elsewhere, including the ones with wireless remotes and side panels, multiple wash modes, and "smart" feature lists, ships in the same bright white regardless of brand. The honest trade-off this category hands you is binary: match your fixture's color, or get the comfort features. Right now, almost nothing in our catalogue lets you have both at once.

The bigger decision

Matching a 20-year-old toilet versus just replacing the whole fixture

If your toilet is bone, almond or biscuit and you want the seat to match, you're really choosing between two different projects, and it's worth being clear-eyed about the cost of each before you decide. The first option is buying the Biscuit M250 and accepting its trade-offs: a manual, cool-water seat, elongated fit only in our data, and no electric comfort features. At $117.30, it's a low-cost way to get a color-matched seat without touching the toilet itself, and it's reversible — if you replace the toilet later, the seat comes off in minutes.

The second option is replacing the toilet entirely and buying whatever seat you actually want in white. A new toilet runs anywhere from roughly $150 for a basic two-piece unit to $500 or more for a comfort-height or one-piece model, plus installation if you're not doing it yourself — call it $250–$700 all-in for most households, before the bidet seat itself. That's obviously a bigger spend than a $117 seat, but it buys you something the Biscuit M250 can't: free choice of any electric seat in the category, in the one color that market has standardized on, on a modern bowl that current manufacturers actually design for.

The honest framing is this: match the old toilet if the rest of the bathroom is staying as-is for years, the toilet itself still functions perfectly, and you're not interested in electric features right now — the Biscuit M250 solves exactly that problem for very little money. Replace the toilet if you were already due for a renovation, if low-flow efficiency or a taller comfort-height bowl matters to you anyway, or if warm water and a heated seat are non-negotiable and you've decided color simply isn't going to win that fight. Neither path is wrong; they're just answering different questions, and knowing which question you're actually asking is most of the decision.

FAQ

Bidet seat colors: common questions

Why are almost all bidet seats only sold in white?

Because the toilets they attach to are almost all white now. Offering a second shell color means a second mold, a second SKU and a second listing for a shrinking pool of buyers whose fixture predates the white-only era — a cost most manufacturers without an existing fixture business don't find worth carrying.

Is Kohler's Biscuit M250 the only non-white bidet seat available?

In our catalogue, yes — it's the only listing sold in a shade other than white. Kohler can offer it because it already manufactures toilets, tanks and other fixtures in Biscuit and other builder shades, giving it a shorter path to a second seat color than a brand with no fixture history.

Does the Biscuit M250 have any electric features?

No. It's a manual, cool-water seat with a quiet-close lid, quick-release hinges and adjustable spray position and pressure, built for elongated bowls. There's no heater, dryer or remote on this listing — the color match comes bundled with the simpler mechanism, not an electric one.

Can I get an electric bidet seat in a color other than white?

Not in our catalogue. The electric seat we cover here, with warm water, a heated seat and a remote, carries no listed color field at all — check the current Amazon listing directly if a specific shade matters to you. Right now, color options and electric comfort features don't overlap in the data we track.
