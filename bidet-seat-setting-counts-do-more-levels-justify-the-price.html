4 Temperatures, 5 Pressure Levels, 5 Nozzle Positions: Do More Settings Justify a Higher Price?

By Ilane TallUpdated November 14, 2026Research-based.

Open three electric bidet seat listings side by side and you'll notice something odd before you've read a single review: the number of settings a manufacturer is willing to print varies wildly, and it doesn't line up with price the way you'd expect. One listing names a precise count for every function — four water temperatures, five pressure levels, five nozzle positions, plus a kid mode and an eco mode. Another names exactly three of everything. A third just says "adjustable" twice and stops there, with no number attached to either word.

We pulled three real listings from our database to test what that pattern actually means: SmartWhale's electric seat at $279.99, HZOV's at $211.00, and RUIFQ's at $319.99. If a published settings count tracked price, the seat with the most detail on its spec sheet should cost the most and the seat with the least detail should cost the least. It doesn't work that way here, and the gap between what a listing says and what you're actually paying for is the whole point of this guide.

One honest note before we go further: we don't own these three seats, and we don't run a testing lab to verify that "5 pressure levels" feels meaningfully different from "3 pressure levels" under your own hand. What follows is a comparison of what each manufacturer has chosen to publish, cross-checked against price and against the pattern of detail (or its absence) across the rest of each listing — not a verdict based on using either seat ourselves.

The short answer

If you just want the verdict

A published count means something

When it's specific and it's everywhere in the listing

SmartWhale names exact numbers for temperature, pressure, nozzle position, heated-seat levels and warm-air levels — five separate counted specs, not one. That density of detail, repeated across an entire listing, is a far stronger signal than any single number on its own. The SmartWhale seat is $279.99.

It means nothing by itself

When price doesn't follow it either way

RUIFQ's listing names no pressure or nozzle count at all, just the word "adjustable" — and it's the most expensive of the three at $319.99. HZOV discloses a clean 3/3/3 and undercuts both at $211.00. A missing number doesn't mean a cheap seat, and a detailed spec sheet doesn't mean an expensive one.

The method

Counting what's actually printed, not what "adjustable" implies

"Adjustable" is doing a lot of quiet work in bidet seat listings. It's true of nearly every electric seat on the market and tells you almost nothing about the actual experience — a dial with two positions is "adjustable" in exactly the same technical sense as a remote with nine. The only way to compare these seats honestly is to ignore the adjective and count the noun: how many discrete levels, positions or modes does the listing actually name, in writing, as a number?

That's a narrower question than it sounds. A listing can bury real detail in a wall of bullet text, and it can pad a thin spec sheet with marketing language that reads as detailed without naming a single figure. So for each of these three seats we went through the published bullet points line by line and recorded only the numbers that were explicitly stated — water temperature levels, water pressure levels, nozzle positions, heated-seat levels, and any named modes. Where a listing didn't give a number, we recorded that as a gap, not as "presumably similar to the others." That distinction is the entire finding of this article, so it's worth sitting with the three listings individually before comparing them.

The actual finding

Does a published number track price at all?

Lay the three side by side and the correlation most shoppers assume — more named settings, higher price — simply isn't there. HZOV discloses a full, consistent 3/3/3 and is the cheapest of the three. SmartWhale discloses the most of anyone, 4/5/5 plus two named modes plus heated-seat and warm-air levels, and sits in the middle of the price range, $40.00 below RUIFQ. RUIFQ discloses the least — no pressure count, no nozzle count, nothing beyond a bare 3-level seat heater — and costs the most of the three. If anything, in this specific trio, the relationship runs backwards: the listing with the least numeric detail is the most expensive one.

That doesn't mean RUIFQ's seat is worse, or that SmartWhale's is better value — we haven't tested either, and price differences this size can come from brand positioning, manufacturing cost, bundled accessories, recent promotions, or a dozen factors a spec sheet never mentions. What it does mean is that a published settings count is a listing-writing decision, not a price signal. Some manufacturers choose to itemize every function because detail sells; others lean on "adjustable" and let the seat's other selling points — included accessories, seat material, warranty length — carry the listing instead. Reviewers consistently note that owners rarely care about the raw count once a seat is installed; they care whether the specific level they land on feels right for them, which a number on a listing page can't actually tell you in advance.

The more useful question isn't "which seat has the biggest number," it's "does this listing give me enough information to know what I'm buying before the box arrives." On that measure, SmartWhale and HZOV both clear the bar in different ways — one through sheer density of detail, one through internal consistency — while RUIFQ's listing leaves two of its core wash functions essentially unspecified. That gap is worth knowing about before you buy, even though it says nothing definitive about which seat performs best in your bathroom. The checklist

How to tell a meaningfully adjustable bidet seat from one that just says the word

You don't need to run your own lab test to shop smarter than "adjustable" alone allows. A few minutes on the listing page gets you most of the way there:

  • Ask for the number, not the adjective. "Adjustable water pressure" tells you there's a dial or a button. "5 pressure levels" or "3 pressure levels" tells you the actual range you'll be choosing from. If a listing uses the word without a number anywhere nearby — the way RUIFQ does for both pressure and nozzle position — treat that as a genuine information gap, not a detail the manufacturer simply forgot to mention.
  • Check whether the detail is consistent across the whole listing, not just one headline bullet. HZOV's listing names a number for every function it has; SmartWhale's does the same and then some. A listing that's specific about one marquee feature but vague everywhere else — a precise nozzle-position count next to an unqualified "adjustable heat," for instance — is worth a second read before you assume the whole product is equally well documented.
  • Look at the product images and the Q&A section for the remote or control panel. A remote or side knob with clearly labeled buttons for each named function is a reasonable physical confirmation that the counted levels actually exist as discrete settings, rather than a single slider the copywriter rounded up into "5 levels" for the listing.
  • Read the one-star and two-star reviews for control complaints. Owners who are frustrated by a confusing panel, a setting that doesn't seem to do anything, or a level count that doesn't match what they feel in use will usually say so directly — and that owner sentiment is a better reality check on a settings claim than the bullet point itself.
  • Don't assume a missing number means a worse seat, or a longer list means a better one. As this comparison shows, RUIFQ's thinner spec sheet comes with the highest price of the three, and HZOV's shorter, consistent list comes with the lowest. The count is a clue about how the listing was written, not a verdict on the seat itself.

None of this is a substitute for matching the seat to your own bathroom and budget. FAQ

Settings counts: common questions

Does a higher number of pressure levels actually feel different?

We can't verify that without physically testing these seats, and we don't run a testing lab. What we can say from the listings themselves is that a higher count gives you more positions to choose between on paper — five settings between "off" and "maximum" instead of three — but whether the gap between adjacent levels is noticeable depends on the seat's pump and valve design, which no listing publishes in enough detail to compare directly.

Why would a $319.99 seat list fewer settings than a $279.99 one?

Price on these listings reflects more than settings count — brand positioning, included accessories, manufacturing cost, seat material, and current promotions all factor in, and none of them show up in a pressure-level number. RUIFQ's $319.99 listing simply chose not to itemize every function the way SmartWhale's $279.99 listing does; that's a difference in how the listing was written, not necessarily in what the seat can do.

Is "adjustable" without a number a red flag?

It's an information gap more than a red flag. Plenty of well-reviewed products describe a feature as "adjustable" without a level count, and it doesn't automatically mean the feature is weak or limited. It does mean you're buying with one less data point than a fully itemized listing gives you, so it's worth checking the Q&A section or recent reviews for owners who mention the actual range before you decide it doesn't matter to you.

Which of these three seats should most people buy?

If a fully itemized spec sheet matters to you, SmartWhale's $279.99 listing gives you the most named detail of the three. If you'd rather pay less for a smaller but fully specified set of options, HZOV's $211.00 listing is the lowest price with every function still numbered. RUIFQ, at $319.99 with the thinnest spec sheet, only makes sense if something else about the listing — not the settings count — is what's drawing you to it.
