TOTO WASHLET S5: Why Sedona Beige Costs $240 More Than Cotton White

Shop TOTO's WASHLET S5 long enough and you'll run into a number that looks like a typo: the Cotton White unit (SW3446#01) lists at $540.00, and the Sedona Beige unit (SW3446#12) — the same model number, same "#01" family, same product line — lists at $780.00. That's $240 more for a color. Not a different heater, not an extra wash mode. A color.

The obvious reaction is to assume there's a hidden feature buried in the beige listing that justifies it, so we read both titles in full before writing a word of this. The Cotton White listing reads: "TOTO® WASHLET® S5 Electronic Bidet Toilet Seat with Instantaneous Water Heating, PREMIST and EWATER+ Wand Cleaning, Elongated, Cotton White - SW3446#01." The Sedona Beige listing reads: "TOTO® WASHLET® S5 Electronic Bidet Toilet Seat with Instantaneous Water Heating, PREMIST and EWATER+ Wand Cleaning, Elongated, Sedona Beige - SW3446#12." Put those two strings side by side and exactly two things differ: the color name, and the trailing two digits of the model number. Everything else — the bowl shape, the heating system, the wand-cleaning tech — is typed identically, in the same order, in both.

One honest note before we go further: we don't run a fake testing lab, and we never claim to. Everything below comes from reading TOTO's own listing titles for both SKUs, comparing the database prices recorded for each, and what's publicly known about how colored plumbing fixtures get made. Where a listing doesn't state something, we say so instead of guessing.

Two features commonly associated with TOTO's higher-end WASHLET tiers — a nightlight and an automatic air deodorizer — aren't named in either title here, so we won't claim they're included or that they're identical between the two colors. Since both SKUs share the same SW3446 base model number and differ only in the suffix tied to color, there's no engineering reason color alone would add or remove an unrelated feature like a nightlight. But we're working from what TOTO actually printed in these two titles, not from what the S-line generally tends to include, and the rule we hold ourselves to is simple: if a listing doesn't state it, we don't assert it. If a nightlight or deodorizer matters to your decision, confirm it directly on the current listing before you buy either color.

So if the stated feature list is identical and the price still differs by $240, the gap has to come from somewhere other than what's bolted onto the seat. That leaves two candidates worth checking honestly: what it actually costs TOTO to produce a colored unit instead of white, and whether a $240 premium is remotely proportional to that cost. We'll take both in turn.

That rules out the easiest explanation for the $240 — that the beige unit simply has more inside it. TOTO isn't charging $780.00 for Sedona Beige because it bundled in a function Cotton White lacks. It's charging more for the identical washlet in a different color. That's a stronger, cleaner version of the question than "is this seat worth $780," because the question here isn't about the seat at all. It's specifically about what a color is worth on top of an otherwise unchanged product — and whether TOTO's $240 number has any real manufacturing basis, or whether it's simply what the market will bear for a less common finish.

Why color costs money in porcelain

What colored porcelain and matched trim actually cost a toilet maker

The glaze isn't optional, and it isn't free

Colored glazes run smaller batches

Vitreous china fixtures get their color from a pigmented glaze fired onto the body at high heat, not from dye mixed into the clay. Cotton White is the default glaze every kiln run is already set up for — it's what the vast majority of toilets, in any brand's lineup, ship in. A color like Sedona Beige requires its own glaze formulation, its own firing run, and historically a smaller production batch, because demand for colored fixtures is a fraction of demand for white. Smaller batches raise the per-unit cost of the glazing step itself, independent of how complex the shape being glazed is.

Matching, not just coloring

Trim and seat plastics have to match the glaze, not just look close

An electric WASHLET seat isn't one material — it's a plastic shell and lid sitting on top of a porcelain-adjacent color story that buyers expect to match the bowl underneath it. Getting a molded plastic seat to match a specific fired-glaze beige, run after run, consistently enough that a showroom pairing looks intentional rather than close-but-off, has historically meant tighter color-matching tolerances and more quality-control rejects than simply molding plastic white. Both of those add cost on the finishing side, on top of whatever the glaze itself costs.

Put those two factors together and a colored WASHLET stops looking like a straightforward "add $X for beige" sticker price. Smaller glaze batches, a dedicated firing run, and tighter color-matching tolerances on the seat's own plastics are all real, specific cost drivers that apply to Sedona Beige and don't apply to Cotton White, which is the default color every production line is already tuned for. None of that is something we can verify from a teardown — TOTO doesn't publish what its glaze runs or color-matching rejects actually cost per unit, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.

What we can do is ask whether $240 is a plausible number for that kind of cost, or whether it's clearly priced well past it. On a $540 base, $240 is a 44% premium — nearly half again the price of the white unit, for a product where the color itself is a glaze and a plastic tint, not a different mechanism. Historically, colored-fixture premiums in plumbing have run real but modest: enough to cover a dedicated run and extra QC, not enough to double the product's effective tier. A 44% jump is large enough that we think it's more honest to describe it as "plausibly includes a real production cost, and also plausibly reflects what TOTO can charge for a less common color" than to claim the $240 is fully explained by what Sedona Beige costs to make. We don't have TOTO's bill of materials, so we can't settle which share of that $240 is cost and which is margin — and we'd rather say that plainly than invent a number that sounds precise.

Beyond this one pair

How the $240 color gap compares to TOTO's own S2 shape premium

TOTO charging a premium for a non-default option isn't new to this brand — we've already documented it on the S2 line, where the round-bowl S2 lists at $39.96 more than the elongated S2 for an otherwise identical feature set. That's a useful yardstick, because it tells us how TOTO prices a premium tied to an actual manufacturing difference: a shorter, differently tooled shell, built in lower volume than the default elongated shape. On a $378.00 elongated base, that $39.96 works out to roughly 10.6% — a real but modest bump, consistent with "this costs a bit more to produce and TOTO passed some of it through."

The S5 color gap looks nothing like that. $240 on a $540 base is 44% — more than four times the proportional size of the S2's bowl-shape premium, for a difference that's arguably simpler to produce than a redesigned shell: a different glaze and a matched plastic tint, on the same mechanism, same shape, same internals. If a genuine shape change that requires its own tooling only commands roughly 11% on this brand's own pricing, a same-shape, same-mechanism color swap commanding 44% is hard to justify purely on production cost. It's the clearest internal evidence we have that TOTO's color premium isn't scaled the same way its shape premium is — which points back toward the color surcharge being, at least in part, a pricing decision rather than a cost pass-through.

Before you pay a color premium

What to check before paying a color premium on any WASHLET model

A color surcharge isn't unique to the S5, and it won't always be $240 or 44%. Before you accept a colored unit's price on any TOTO WASHLET model, run through the same checks that got us to the number above:

  • Read both titles word for word. Lay the white listing and the colored listing side by side, the way we did here, and confirm the only stated differences are color and the model suffix. If a colored listing names an extra feature the white one doesn't, the premium is partly buying that feature, not just the color — and that's a different, easier-to-justify gap than a pure color surcharge.
  • Calculate the premium as a percentage, not just a dollar figure. $240 sounds like a flat add-on; on this $540 base it's actually a 44% jump. The percentage tells you whether you're looking at a modest finish surcharge or close to doubling what you'd pay for the same mechanism in white.
  • Compare it against that brand's own shape premium, if one exists. TOTO's own S2 round-vs-elongated gap — a change that actually requires different tooling — runs about 10.6%. If a color premium on the same brand runs several times larger than its own shape premium, cost alone is unlikely to be the full explanation.
  • Confirm your bowl's actual color before ordering either way. "Beige" covers a range of porcelain shades across toilet generations, and TOTO's Sedona Beige is a specific, named match. Our guide to reading a seat's stated dimensions covers the same discipline applied to fit — the short version for color is the same: check the listing's exact name against your own fixture rather than eyeballing it from a photo.

If none of that changes your answer and you still want the exact bowl-matched look, $240 is simply what TOTO charges for it on this model today — our job here is making sure you're paying it with eyes open, not assuming it's hiding an extra feature that isn't actually there. And if the whole point of upgrading is reliable installation day one, our guide to what "easy installation" actually means is worth a read regardless of which color you land on, since neither S5 listing spells out hardware details beyond the color name.

It's also worth knowing this pattern isn't universal across the category. Combier prices its round and elongated seats identically — no premium either direction for a genuine shape difference — and Kohler's own PureWash lineup shows that within one manufacturer's catalogue, price gaps more often track a specific named feature than a cosmetic choice like color. TOTO's Sedona Beige surcharge is a real, current number on this specific pair of listings; it isn't evidence that every brand prices color the same way, and it isn't evidence that TOTO prices every finish option the same way either. FAQ

TOTO S5 Sedona Beige vs Cotton White: common questions

What actually differs between the TOTO WASHLET S5 Sedona Beige and Cotton White?

Based on TOTO's own listing titles, only the color, the trailing model suffix (SW3446#12 for Sedona Beige, SW3446#01 for Cotton White), and the price. Instantaneous water heating, PREMIST bowl-wetting, EWATER+ wand cleaning and the elongated bowl fit are named identically on both listings.

Why does Sedona Beige cost $240 more than Cotton White?

We can't see TOTO's manufacturing costs directly, but colored vitreous-china fixtures have historically required their own glaze formulation, a dedicated firing run at lower volume than the default white, and tighter color-matching tolerances on the seat's plastics to pair convincingly with the bowl. Those are real cost drivers. Whether they add up to the full $240, or whether part of that figure is simply what TOTO can charge for a less common finish, isn't something TOTO publishes — and we won't invent a split we can't verify.

Is a 44% color premium normal for TOTO?

It's large relative to what TOTO charges for an actual manufacturing difference elsewhere in its lineup. The S2's round-versus-elongated premium — a change that requires different tooling — runs about 10.6% on top of the elongated price. A same-shape, same-mechanism color swap commanding roughly four times that proportional premium is hard to justify on production cost alone, which suggests pricing strategy, not just manufacturing cost, is doing some of the work.

Should I just buy Cotton White and have it refinished or wait for a cheaper beige?

We don't have data on refinishing costs or timelines, so we won't speculate on that route. What we can say is that if your existing bowl is genuinely Sedona Beige, the colored S5 is the only one of these two listings that will match it, and $240 is the current cost of that match on Amazon — confirm the live price before assuming it still holds.
